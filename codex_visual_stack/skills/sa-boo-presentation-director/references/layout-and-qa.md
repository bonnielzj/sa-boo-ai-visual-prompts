## 1. Layout Reasoning

Start with the rhetorical job:

| Purpose | Spatial consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Introduce | Minimal information, strong title, clear identity signal |
| Diagnose | Evidence first, annotations second, conclusion visible |
| Prove | One dominant fact, image, chart, plan, or comparison |
| Compare | Strong separation, shared baseline, explicit recommendation |
| Transition | Low density, high contrast, one sentence or image |
| Recommend | Preferred option visually dominant, reasons secondary |
| Close | Concrete decision, next action, owner, or timing |

Use content hierarchy to choose the canvas division. Equal columns imply equal
importance. Unequal columns create direction and should be the default when
one item leads.

Spatial relationships communicate meaning:

- overlap suggests connection or layering;
- isolation suggests importance;
- adjacency suggests comparison or sequence;
- cropping at the edge creates scale and motion;
- generous whitespace creates emphasis;
- tight grouping creates a single conceptual unit.

## 2. Slide Archetypes

Use these only as fallbacks after defining the purpose.

### Cover or section pause

- one title or statement;
- brand/project signal in the first viewport;
- image or material cue when relevant;
- a concise bridge when the chapter or context would otherwise be unclear;
- omit explanation only when the sequence already makes the relationship legible.

### Hero statement

- one conclusion;
- the minimum supporting copy needed to understand the conclusion;
- large empty field or one controlled image.

### Evidence-led

- chart, plan, render, photograph, or table is the largest object;
- annotations explain only what the audience must notice;
- conclusion is visible without reading every label.

### Image-led asymmetric

- size the image around its subject and evidential role; it may fill the canvas;
- keep text in an optically deliberate field or quiet part of the image;
- crop and focal point support the text position.

### Metric or quote

- one number, phrase, or client insight dominates;
- context and source are small but legible;
- avoid surrounding the metric with multiple cards.

### Comparison

- shared criteria and scale;
- differences are spatially and typographically obvious;
- recommendation is not hidden in body copy.

### Process or timeline

- direction and phase changes are clear;
- use the fewest steps that preserve meaning;
- do not reduce a complex process to decorative arrows.

### Dense evidence

- commit to density with a strict grid;
- use repeated alignment and restrained color;
- split the slide when items are not directly comparable.

## 3. Deck Rhythm

Plan the deck as a sequence, not independent pages.

- Avoid more than two consecutive slides with the same silhouette.
- Alternate light and dark only when the switch marks a real chapter or
  emphasis change.
- Follow a dense slide with a simpler synthesis, recommendation, or pause.
- Carry editorial constants such as type roles, margins, alignment, and navigation
  across the sequence. Repeat a graphic motif only when it serves the subject;
  creative worlds may retain distinct styles bridged by context and sequencing.
- Use section slides to reset attention in longer decks.
- A contact sheet should reveal a deliberate cadence of open, medium, and
  dense pages.

## 4. Typography And Density

- Establish viewing mode first: projected talk and a privately circulated
  editorial PDF need different type scales. Do not impose universal minima.
- Specify distinct roles for display, reading text, navigation and captions.
  Test the real longest Chinese and Latin strings in the actual fonts.
- Use type contrast intentionally: family, width, case, weight, tracking,
  line length and placement all contribute. Increasing size alone is insufficient.
- For bilingual work, assign unequal complementary roles; avoid mechanically
  duplicating every line in two languages at equal weight.
- Check that both languages actually contribute: a product or case title in
  English can shape the composition, with Chinese giving precise local context.
  Small uppercase navigation alone is not a bilingual title system. Preserve
  needed introductions and connective explanations; English can provide an active
  compositional counterweight rather than become a reduced translation footer.
- Do not infer serif type, italics or giant display text from the words
  'premium' or 'editorial'. Derive the family and scale from the actual reference
  and current feedback. Establish hierarchy through weight, spacing, line length
  and placement before adding another family. Likewise, a rejection of excessive
  serif does not mean ban all serif; a rejection of all-sans does not mean add
  decorative serif to every page. Test bounded pairings on real imagery.
- Shape line breaks and optical spacing. Shorten copy before cramming it.
- Judge captions at expected reading size. Small metadata must remain useful;
  it is not decorative texture or a substitute for the argument.

Suggested body-word budgets:

| Slide type | Body words |
| --- | ---: |
| Cover/transition | 0-20 |
| Hero/metric | 0-35 |
| Image-led | 15-55 |
| Comparison | 30-90 |
| Dense evidence | 60-130 |

These are review thresholds, not reasons to pad a slide.

## 5. Visual QA Rubric

Use the rubric for a client-ready deck or a requested assessment; a quick preview
needs targeted visual judgment, not a scored report. Score applicable dimensions
from 1 to 5. Any applicable score below 3 is blocking for client-ready release; the
deck should average at least 4 with no blocking structural defect. Assess
editability only when the deliverable promises editability; it does not block an
image-first preview or PDF-only contract without that promise.

### Content

1. Unclear, inaccurate, or badly structured.
2. Weak focus and awkward organization.
3. Clear but under-edited or poorly supported.
4. Clear, relevant, and supported.
5. Precise, persuasive, and text/visual evidence reinforce each other.

### Composition

1. Overlap, clipping, broken alignment, or unreadable contrast.
2. Mechanically arranged with weak hierarchy.
3. Functional but generic.
4. Balanced, intentional, and appropriate to the message.
5. Distinctive, controlled, and immediately legible.

### Coherence

1. Chaotic sequence.
2. Understandable sections with weak transitions.
3. Logical sequence but limited pacing or context.
4. Strong flow, context, and section rhythm.
5. Memorable narrative with deliberate pacing and conclusion.

### Consistency

1. Conflicting type, color, or layout systems.
2. Repeated local inconsistencies.
3. Consistent but monotonous.
4. Cohesive with controlled variation.
5. Recognizable visual system with purposeful variation.

### Editability

1. Critical content is flattened or impossible to modify.
2. Major edits require reconstruction.
3. Main text and images are editable.
4. Components, charts, and styles are sensibly structured.
5. Theme, masters, components, and object naming support repeated use.

## 6. Common Failure Patterns

- Same title/body/grid silhouette on every slide.
- Symmetric 50/50 split used without equal content importance.
- Every idea placed in a rounded rectangle.
- Decorative line under every heading.
- Dense copy disguised with attractive typography.
- Image added after layout, producing arbitrary crop and focal conflict.
- Full-bleed rectangles covering template backgrounds or master chrome.
- Repeated use of the same image.
- Inconsistent page margins or title anchors.
- Charts used as decoration without a visible conclusion.
- Repeated technical rendering without inspecting composition or addressing feedback.
- White JPEG boxes on dark backgrounds, halos, clipped logos, or approximate retyped marks.
- Dark/light alternation mistaken for rhythm; empty space mistaken for sophistication.
- Generic font-size rules overriding the actual source typography and viewing mode.
- Structural checks treated as visual-design approval.

The design goal is not novelty on every slide. It is clear hierarchy,
controlled variation, and a deck-level rhythm that makes the argument easier
to understand.
